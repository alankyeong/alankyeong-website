## The named source behind the regional figure

NIQ (formerly Nielsen), in research published 7 November 2025 covering Southeast Asian consumer behaviour, found that 53% of consumers surveyed cited cost specifically as the main barrier to making sustainable purchases — a precise, named-organisation figure rather than a general assertion about consumer indifference ([NIQ, "Southeast Asia's consumers are redefining value amidst headwinds, powering a $5 trillion consumption future by 2035"](https://nielseniq.com/global/en/news-center/2025/southeast-asias-consumers-are-redefining-value-amidst-headwinds-powering-a-5-trillion-consumption-future-by-2035/)). This is a genuine primary source — NIQ's own press release, quoting Bain & Company partner Praneeth Yendamuri directly — and it frames the barrier in more specific terms than a blanket "consumers don't really mean it" narrative: the same release describes consumers as increasingly "brand agnostic," "deliberate in their choices," and "balancing affordability with aspiration," alongside a broader finding that 43% of regional consumers report cutting back on non-household expenditures amid economic uncertainty.

## What a separate, older but methodologically disclosed survey adds

A separate source, Rakuten Insight's "Sustainability speaks" report (fieldwork late 2023, though this specific caveat matters and is addressed below), found regional variation worth noting: Indonesia (68%), Thailand (62%), and the Philippines and Vietnam (61%) ranked as the most "action-oriented" Southeast Asian markets in translating stated sustainability concern into actual purchasing behaviour, with the report attributing this to those markets' closer proximity to visible climate change impacts ([Rakuten Insight, "Sustainability speaks: Unveiling consumer mindsets across APAC"](https://rakuten.today/blog/sustainability-speaks-unveiling-consumer-mindsets-across-apac.html)). The same report found 67.9% of consumers across surveyed APAC markets expressing readiness to pay more for sustainable products, up marginally from 67.6% the prior year, with Gen Z (72%) and Millennials (70%) showing meaningfully higher willingness than Gen X (59%) or Boomers (48%).

This Rakuten Insight data is flagged **Partially Verified** on a specific and important basis: the underlying fieldwork appears to be from December 2023, even though it is being cited in ongoing 2026 commentary as current. This is precisely the kind of fieldwork-date versus republication-date gap this article's research process was instructed to catch and disclose rather than pass through silently — the regional ranking and willingness-to-pay figures cited here should be understood as reflecting attitudes from late 2023, not necessarily 2026, even where secondary sources present them without that qualification.

## The gap between the two sources, and what it suggests

Read together, NIQ's directly-dated November 2025 finding (53% citing cost as the primary barrier) and Rakuten Insight's late-2023 finding (67.9% expressing willingness to pay more) are not strictly contradictory — a consumer can genuinely express willingness to pay more in the abstract while still citing cost as the practical barrier when actually purchasing — but the roughly two-year gap between the two data collection periods, and the shift toward NIQ's more recent framing of "brand agnostic" and "deliberate" consumer behaviour amid documented spending cutbacks (43% reducing non-household expenditure), suggests the more current picture may show a narrower gap between stated willingness and actual purchase behaviour than the older Rakuten figures alone would imply. This is this article's own inference connecting the two data points, not a claim either source makes directly.

## What the evidence does not resolve

Neither source directly addresses the specific mechanism this article's earlier draft asserted — that vague sustainability claims convert poorly while specific, certified claims convert well. That claim-specificity thesis remains directionally plausible as general marketing logic, but this research did not locate a Southeast Asia-specific study directly testing claim-specificity against conversion or willingness-to-pay, and it should not be presented as an established finding without that direct evidence.

## What this means for sustainability positioning

The defensible, evidence-supported position is narrower than "consumers say they care but don't follow through." NIQ's directly-sourced 2025 data indicates cost is the specific, named barrier for a bare majority (53%) of Southeast Asian consumers — not indifference to sustainability itself, and not primarily distrust of sustainability claims, which is a different and more actionable diagnosis than the vaguer "say-do gap" framing common in older, more general sustainability marketing commentary. A brand whose sustainable product carries a meaningful price premium is running directly into the specific, named obstacle NIQ's research identifies; a brand that can close or substantially narrow that price gap, rather than simply making its sustainability claims more specific or more certified, is addressing the barrier this research actually documents.

## Two rankings, two very different stories

Population-level AI usage data circulating in 2026 is regularly used to build country league tables, and Singapore performs well on them: Stanford's 2026 AI Index Report -- the field's most widely cited annual benchmark, produced by Stanford's Institute for Human-Centered AI -- puts Singapore's population-level generative AI adoption at 61%, explicitly named as one of the countries that "outpace what income would predict" ([Stanford HAI, "The 2026 AI Index Report," Economy chapter](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy)). Global population adoption reached 53% within three years of generative AI's mass-market introduction, per the same report, faster than the personal computer or the internet.

China's position on that same population-adoption measure is genuinely harder to pin down than commentary on this topic usually admits. Stanford's own report, in the material available for this article, does not publish a specific China population-adoption percentage alongside its Singapore, UAE, and US figures -- and a widely circulated secondary figure claiming Chinese population-level usage at 16% could not be traced to Stanford's report or any other primary dataset this research could independently verify, so it has been dropped rather than repeated. What Stanford's report does state directly, and precisely, is a different and more specific metric: the share of *regular workplace* AI users -- as distinct from general population usage -- "exceeds 80% in India, China, Nigeria, the United Arab Emirates, and Saudi Arabia" ([Stanford HAI, 2026 AI Index Report, Economy chapter](https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy)). That is a genuinely different question from population-level adoption, and on it, China sits among the highest-usage countries in the world, not near the bottom.

This is precisely the methodology trap this article set out to examine. A "population adoption" ranking and a "regular workplace user" ranking are not interchangeable, they are not always published for the same set of countries, and conflating them -- treating a missing or unverifiable population figure as evidence of low adoption, when the workplace-usage figure for the same country is among the world's highest -- produces exactly the kind of misleading country league table this article argues against relying on.

That comparison measures the wrong thing for anyone trying to understand *enterprise* AI maturity specifically -- which is the question that actually matters for a business deciding where AI-driven decision-making is furthest along. Neither population usage nor workplace usage rates say much about whether organisations have moved AI beyond pilots into production systems that actually change how decisions get made.

## What the enterprise-specific data actually shows

On enterprise deployment specifically, the most directly comparable and most rigorously disclosed data available is a matched pair: the Deloitte AI Institute's "State of AI in the Enterprise" survey covering Singapore, and the HKU-Deloitte China AI Adoption Index covering mainland China and Hong Kong — both fielded by the same research organisation within months of each other in 2026, which makes them unusually comparable.

The Deloitte Singapore survey found that 32% of Singapore respondents had moved 40% or more of their AI pilots into production, against a 25% global average, from a survey of over 3,000 director-to-C-suite leaders including 75 in Singapore specifically ([Deloitte AI Institute, "State of AI in the Enterprise: The Untapped Edge," 3 February 2026](https://www.deloitte.com/southeast-asia/en/about/press-room/agentic-and-physical-ai-set-for-rapid-growth-in-singapore-in-the-next-two-years.html)). The HKU-Deloitte China index, based on a survey of over 100 C-suite leaders across mainland China and Hong Kong, found a similarly cautious picture: more than one-third of organisations still in the exploration phase, 56% in "limited implementation" yielding only localised benefits, 23% reporting measurable financial impact, and just 4% describing their AI deployment as genuinely transformational ([Deloitte and HKU, "HKU and Deloitte China AI Adoption Index 2026"](https://www.deloitte.com/cn/en/services/consulting/perspectives/hku-and-deloitte-china-ai-adoption-index-2026.html)).

Read side by side, these two surveys — same research house, same year, broadly comparable senior-leader sampling — don't support a clean "China lags, Singapore leads" story. Singapore's 32% pilot-to-production figure and China's 27% (23% measurable impact plus 4% transformational, the closest comparable band in the China index's categories) sit within a similar range, both well short of majority deployment. The gap the population-usage rankings suggest simply doesn't show up at the enterprise level once the comparison uses matched methodology.

## What the China index adds that the Singapore survey doesn't

The HKU-Deloitte China index goes further than the Singapore survey in diagnosing *why* enterprise deployment lags ambition, and the diagnosis is instructive precisely because it locates the constraint away from technology. The index explicitly attributes the gap to "organisational barriers—silos, cultural resistance, and poor data—rather than technical limits," and separately finds that 9% of AI projects in the surveyed China/Hong Kong sample delivered *negative* returns, attributed to "hidden costs and disruptions" rather than the technology underperforming outright. The top-cited barriers were siloed departments and cultural resistance, limited understanding of AI capabilities among decision-makers, absence of early "quick wins" to build momentum, unforeseen implementation complexity, and unclear business cases going in.

This is a genuinely useful list because none of it is specific to China — the same barriers (organisational silos, unclear business cases, data quality) show up in essentially every rigorous enterprise AI survey published in 2026 regardless of geography, including the broader global McKinsey and NVIDIA surveys covering the same period. What the China-specific framing adds is confirmation that these are the *dominant* barriers even in a market with strong government AI investment and rapid model development — evidence against a common assumption that China's AI enterprise gap, where one exists, is primarily a technology-access or investment-scale problem rather than an organisational-execution one.

## Where a genuine regional difference does show up

The one place a real, structural difference between Northern Asian and Southeast Asian enterprise AI activity does appear in the 2026 data is investment scale and agent-scaling ambition, not deployment maturity. KPMG's Global AI Pulse survey, fielded between 17 February and 17 March 2026 across 20 countries with 2,110 C-suite respondents, found the Asia-Pacific region (defined by KPMG to include mainland China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan) planning the highest average AI investment of any region — a weighted US$245 million per organisation, against US$207 million for the broader Americas grouping — and the highest rate of AI agent scaling at 49%, against 46% for the Americas and 42% for EMEA ([KPMG International, "Three out of four global leaders will prioritize AI investment," 31 March 2026](https://kpmg.com/xx/en/media/press-releases/2026/03/kpmg-global-ai-pulse-survey.html)). This is investment intent and ambition running ahead of actual deployment maturity — a genuinely different pattern from Singapore's more measured, closer-to-production 32% figure, and worth reading as two different postures (ambitious investment versus disciplined deployment) rather than one market simply being "ahead" of the other.

## What this means for planning an AI programme across these markets

For a business coordinating AI strategy across both Northern Asian and Southeast Asian operations, the practical implication is to stop using population-level usage rankings as a proxy for enterprise readiness in either direction — they measure something close to irrelevant to the deployment-maturity question. The more useful planning input is the organisational-barrier list the HKU-Deloitte China index identifies, since it's corroborated by comparable global research and appears to apply regardless of which specific market an operation sits in: the constraint on moving from pilot to production is overwhelmingly about data readiness, organisational ownership, and realistic business-case scoping, not about which country's population uses ChatGPT more often.

## A note on comparability

It's worth being explicit about a limitation in this comparison rather than letting it pass silently. The Deloitte Singapore survey and the HKU-Deloitte China index are not the same instrument — they use different question sets and different maturity categorisations, so the "32% versus 27%" comparison drawn above is an approximation built by mapping roughly equivalent bands across two related but distinct surveys, not a direct like-for-like statistic from a single unified study. Both surveys share Deloitte as a common research partner and were fielded in the same year with broadly similar senior-executive sampling, which makes the comparison more defensible than most cross-study comparisons in this space, but a business making a significant resourcing decision based on the precise gap between these two figures should treat the general pattern (both markets sitting well short of majority production deployment, with similar organisational barriers) as the reliable takeaway, rather than the exact percentage-point difference between them.

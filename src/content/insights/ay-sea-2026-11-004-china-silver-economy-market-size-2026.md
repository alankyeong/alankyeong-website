## The demographic facts aren't in dispute

Start with what's genuinely settled. China's National Bureau of Statistics, at a State Council Information Office briefing on 26 January 2026, confirmed the national population stood at 1.40489 billion at end-2025, a decrease of 3.39 million from end-2024, with 7.92 million births against 11.31 million deaths during the year and a natural population growth rate of minus 2.41 per thousand ([NBS, via China's State Council Information Office, 26 January 2026](http://english.scio.gov.cn/m/pressroom/2026-01/26/content_118301853.html)). This is a primary government data source, and the direction it describes — a shrinking, aging population — isn't seriously contested by any source reviewed for this article. Various sources put China's population aged 60 and above at somewhere between 320 million and 323 million by end-2025, representing 22-23% of the total population, with consistent projections that this cohort will exceed 400 million by the mid-2030s ([CKGSB Knowledge, 22 April 2026](https://english.ckgsb.edu.cn/knowledge/article/the-silver-economy-chinas-next-growth-engine/); [China Economic Review](https://chinaeconomicreview.com/severely-silver/); [HKTDC Research](https://research.hktdc.com/en/article/MjI5NDg4MTY1OQ)).

## Where the sources genuinely diverge

The demographic scale is agreed. The economic size of the "silver economy" this demographic supports is not, and the spread is wide enough to be worth examining directly rather than picking whichever figure sounds most impressive. Two independent state-media wire reports -- Xinhua and, separately, Time magazine citing China National Radio -- both describe China's silver economy as "around 7 trillion yuan" currently, rising to "around 30 trillion yuan" by 2035, in each case reporting the same government framing rather than independent estimates of their own ([Xinhua, "China's silver economy unlocks potential," 26 July 2024](https://english.news.cn/20240726/573decc00cfb4e5e9b7be2b6606a7f2e/c.html); [Time, "China Unveils 'Silver Economy' Plan for Aging Population"](https://time.com/6555949/china-silver-economy-aging-population-plan/)). This pairing traces back to the State Council's January 2024 policy document on developing the silver economy, and the consistency across two independently reported wire accounts of the same government figure is enough to treat the 7-trillion-now, 30-trillion-by-2035 pairing as the closest thing to an official Chinese government estimate available in English-language reporting.

A separate, explicitly distinct estimate comes from academic rather than government sources. The Fudan University Institute on Aging projects a more conservative RMB 19.1 trillion (roughly USD 2.69 trillion) by 2035 under a moderate per-capita consumption growth scenario, framed specifically as 27.8% of total consumption and 9.6% of GDP by that point -- a figure reported independently and consistently by both Global Times and China Daily, two separate outlets both explicitly naming the Fudan Institute as the source ([Global Times, "China's solution to proactively developing the silver economy," 17 September 2025](https://www.globaltimes.cn/page/202509/1343764.shtml); [China Daily, "Develop silver economy to lift well-being of seniors," 12 January 2026](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202601/12/WS696451a0a310d6866eb332fa.html)). This is a genuinely separate estimate from the government's own 30-trillion figure, not a restatement of it -- the two differ by more than a third for the same target year, and the difference is worth taking at face value as a real disagreement between the government's own framing and an independent academic institute's more conservative model, rather than assuming one must be an error.

A third figure, describing a "government-linked estimate" of RMB 9 trillion for 2025 rising to the same RMB 30 trillion by 2035, appeared in a single source that did not name the specific originating body or document ([a market-overview site describing itself as citing government-linked research](https://ashleydudarenok.com/silver-economy/)). Because this specific RMB 9 trillion figure could not be corroborated against a second independent source, it has been removed from this article rather than retained at a lower confidence level, consistent with the standard applied throughout this correction cycle.

None of the retained figures is fabricated -- each traces to a named, identifiable source. What they disagree on isn't the direction of the trend but the *scope and methodology* of what's being counted: the government's own 7-to-30-trillion framing versus Fudan's independently modelled, more conservative 2035 projection. This is a genuinely important distinction for market sizing, and it should be read as two different institutions' different estimates, not as noise to be averaged away.

## What the spending-pattern research adds, and where it should be trusted more

On digital behaviour specifically, official data provides firm ground, corroborated across multiple independent outlets citing the same underlying report. The China Internet Network Information Center's (CNNIC) 56th Statistical Report on China's Internet Development, released 21 July 2025, found 161 million internet users aged 60 and above as of June 2025, with internet penetration among that age group reaching 52% -- a figure reported consistently by China's own State Council English-language portal, CGTN, and the Chinese Academy of Sciences, all citing the same CNNIC report directly ([China's State Council, 21 July 2025](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202507/21/content_WS687dd259c6d0868f4e8f4501.html); [CGTN, 22 July 2025](https://news.cgtn.com/news/2025-07-22/CNNIC-report-China-has-over-1-12-billion-internet-users-1FcXV0pkpos/p.html)). Separately, CNNIC's own data, reported via China's State Council portal, found nearly 70% of internet users aged 60 and above shop online ([China's State Council, 18 January 2025](https://english.www.gov.cn/archive/statistics/202501/18/content_WS678ae7f6c6d0868f4e8eef08.html)). Both figures directly contradict a "technophobic elderly" stereotype some brand strategy still assumes by default, and both are Verified Primary via the government's own English-language statistical portal rather than a secondary summary of it.

Two further claims from the earlier version of this article -- a specific "1.5x urban-versus-rural retiree spending" multiple, and a generational split between "mid-60s" and "late-70s+" Chinese seniors -- could not be corroborated against a second independent source in this correction cycle and have been removed rather than retained on the strength of a single citation. The directional point behind them -- that urban, digitally engaged Chinese seniors behave differently from rural or much older seniors -- remains plausible and consistent with the internet-penetration data above, but the specific multiple and the specific generational cutoffs are not repeated here without better sourcing.

## What this means for a China consumer strategy

The practical lesson from the range of market-size estimates isn't that the data is too unreliable to use -- it's that any China silver-economy figure needs to be paired with a question about which institution produced it and under what methodology before it's useful for planning. The government's own 7-to-30-trillion framing and Fudan's independently modelled, more conservative 19.1-trillion 2035 projection are both legitimate, both traceable to a named institution, and both worth citing -- but they should be cited as what they are: two different institutions' estimates, not a single settled number.

The more consistently supported finding across the sources retained here is the digital-behaviour point: 161 million Chinese internet users are aged 60 and above, more than half of that age group is online, and nearly 70% of older netizens shop online -- all Verified Primary via China's own government statistical portal. A "technophobic elderly" assumption embedded in brand strategy for this market is simply not supported by the government's own data, and any positioning built on that assumption is working against evidence the Chinese government itself has published.

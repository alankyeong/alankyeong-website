## The scope question most Asia-based teams get wrong first

The EU AI Act's territorial reach is broader than "does my company have an EU office," and this is the first misunderstanding worth correcting directly. Under Article 50 of the Act, transparency obligations apply to providers of AI systems "irrespective of whether those providers are established or located within the EU or in a third country," and providers established outside the EU are also subject to the Act's provisions if the output of their AI system is used in the EU ([European Commission, "Transparency obligations under Article 50 of the AI Act" -- official FAQ](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)). A Shanghai- or Singapore-based company with no EU subsidiary can still fall within scope if an AI system it operates produces output -- a recommendation, a decision, a generated document -- that ends up used within the Union. This is a materially wider net than the "do we have EU operations" question most companies ask first.

## What a political agreement is, and isn't -- and getting the 2026 timeline right

Getting the sequence of events precise matters in a legal context, and it's worth stating plainly: a political agreement is not itself an amendment to the law, and an amendment does not take legal effect until it enters into force. The Digital Omnibus on AI -- a package of simplification measures -- was formally adopted as a Commission proposal on 19 November 2025. EU co-legislators (the Council and European Parliament) reached political agreement on the proposal on 7 May 2026. That agreement then had to go through the EU's formal adoption process before becoming binding law: the resulting AI Omnibus Regulation entered into force on 27 July 2026 ([European Commission, "AI Act" -- official policy page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai); [European Commission, "AI Omnibus enters into force" -- official news release, 27 July 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/ai-omnibus-enters-force)). None of the AI Omnibus's changes had binding legal effect between the 7 May political agreement and the 27 July entry into force -- a distinction the earlier version of this article did not draw clearly enough, describing 7 May itself as the date an "amendment" changed the deadlines.

The AI Omnibus's core effect, once in force, was a staggered deferral of the Act's toughest obligations. High-risk AI systems under Annex III (the broad, use-based category covering most enterprise AI applications) now apply from 2 December 2027, rather than the original 2 August 2026 date. High-risk AI systems embedded in already-regulated products (Annex I -- machinery, toys, lifts, and similar) now apply from 2 August 2028 ([European Commission, "AI Act" official policy page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)). Both extensions are confirmed directly on the European Commission's own policy page.

## What did not move, and applied on schedule

What did not move is the detail most likely to be missed by a team that heard "the AI Act got delayed" as a blanket statement. Two separate categories of obligation applied on their original schedule, unaffected by the Omnibus.

General-purpose AI (GPAI) obligations -- covering transparency toward downstream providers and respect for copyright, plus additional safety and security rules for the most advanced models -- became applicable on 2 August 2025, per the Commission's own application timeline ([European Commission, "AI Act" official policy page](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)).

Article 50 transparency obligations -- covering disclosure when a person interacts with an AI system, labelling of deepfakes, and machine-readable marking of AI-generated content -- applied from 2 August 2026, exactly as originally scheduled, with only one narrow exception: providers of AI systems already placed on the market before 2 August 2026 have a limited grace period, until 2 December 2026, specifically for the content-marking and detection obligation under Article 50(2). Content generated before 2 August 2026 does not need to be labelled retroactively ([European Commission, "Transparency obligations under Article 50 of the AI Act" -- official FAQ, last updated 24 July 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)).

Separately, and specifically relevant to enforcement rather than the underlying obligations: the enforcement powers of the AI Office and Member States' national competent authorities themselves began applying from 2 August 2026, covering prohibited-practice enforcement, GPAI-obligation enforcement, and Article 50 transparency enforcement together, per the Commission's own enforcement-framework page ([European Commission, "The enforcement framework of the AI Act" -- official policy page, last updated 24 August 2026](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/enforcement-ai-act)). In plain terms: the GPAI obligations have applied since August 2025, but the enforcement machinery the AI Office uses to act on non-compliance became fully operational a year later, in August 2026 -- a distinction worth understanding rather than treating "applies from" and "enforceable from" as interchangeable.

## What enforcement actually looks like, and what it costs

The AI Office holds direct investigative and sanctioning powers over GPAI models specifically: it can send requests for information, require providers to grant model access for evaluation, request corrective measures, and -- if it establishes an intentional or negligent breach -- recommend the Commission impose penalties. Separately, national competent authorities in each Member State enforce the rules for other AI systems, and the European Data Protection Supervisor enforces the rules for AI systems used by EU institutions themselves ([European Commission, "The enforcement framework of the AI Act"](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/enforcement-ai-act)).

The penalty structure scales with severity and with the offending company's size, not a flat fee: prohibited AI practices carry the highest exposure, up to EUR 35 million or 7% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher. Breaches of GPAI-model obligations carry fines of up to EUR 15 million or 3% of global turnover. Failures specific to AI systems (as opposed to GPAI models) carry fines up to EUR 7.5 million or 1% of turnover ([European Commission, "The enforcement framework of the AI Act"](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/enforcement-ai-act)). Because these figures are pegged to global turnover rather than EU-specific revenue, a large Asia-headquartered multinational's potential exposure scales with its worldwide revenue the moment it falls within scope -- not merely its European revenue.

## The provider-versus-deployer distinction that determines which obligations actually apply

A further practical distinction is worth making explicit, since it determines which specific obligations bind a given company. Under Article 50, "providers" are those who develop an AI system (or have it developed) and place it on the EU market or put it into service under their own name -- and this applies "irrespective of whether those providers are established or located within the EU or in a third country." "Deployers" are those using an AI system under their own authority, excluding purely personal use ([European Commission, "Transparency obligations under Article 50 of the AI Act"](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act)). A deployer's specific transparency obligations under Article 50 are narrower than a provider's: deployers must inform people when emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation systems are used on them, and must clearly label deepfakes and AI-generated text published on matters of public interest, unless that content underwent genuine human review or editorial control. Providers carry the broader set of design-and-development obligations, including ensuring AI-generated outputs carry machine-readable marks. Most Asia-based companies building products on top of a third-party foundation model, rather than training and releasing their own, are more likely to sit in the deployer category for that underlying model -- though a company that substantially modifies a model, or that develops and places its own AI system on the EU market, takes on provider-level obligations for that system regardless of where it is headquartered.

## What an Asia-based multinational should actually prioritise now

Given this corrected timeline, the practical prioritisation for an Asia-based multinational with any EU-facing AI usage is specific rather than general. First, confirm whether any AI system in use -- including third-party tools and vendor platforms, not just internally built systems -- produces output used within the EU, since Article 50's own language confirms this alone can trigger obligations regardless of where the company is headquartered. Second, treat the Article 50 transparency obligations, applicable since 2 August 2026, and the GPAI obligations, applicable since 2 August 2025, as the current compliance reality rather than a future deadline -- both are live law today, with active enforcement machinery as of August 2026. Third, use the confirmed extended runway on high-risk Annex III obligations (now 2 December 2027) and Annex I obligations (now 2 August 2028) to build toward compliance deliberately, tracking official Commission guidance directly rather than third-party compliance summaries, given how much legal precision matters in a regulatory context where a proposal, a political agreement, and an entered-into-force amendment are three genuinely different things with three different legal statuses.

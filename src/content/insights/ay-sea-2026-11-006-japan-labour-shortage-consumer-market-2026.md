## The shortage that shows up in two very different numbers

Japan's labour shortage is usually covered as a cost and productivity problem for employers, and the underlying numbers support that framing. Teikoku Databank's April 2026 survey of 23,083 firms (10,538 valid responses, fielded 16-30 April) found 50.6% of companies reporting a shortage of full-time staff -- down 0.8 points from April 2025, but above 50% for a fourth consecutive April ([Makerstations, "Employment in Japan 2026," citing Teikoku Databank](https://www.makerstations.io/employment-in-japan-statistics/)). The same source reports 441 bankruptcies attributed to staffing shortages in fiscal 2025, a third consecutive record year and the first fiscal year above 400 -- with construction accounting for 112 of those cases (25.4%), road freight transport for 55, and elderly welfare services for 22.

Set against that, the same labour shortage is producing wage growth Japan has not seen in decades. The average rate of headline wage increase agreed in the 2025 Shunto spring wage negotiations was 5.25%, a historically high figure, with base pay increases averaging 3.7% and, notably, that wage growth spreading to small and medium enterprises rather than staying concentrated in large corporations ([OECD, "Key Policy Insights: OECD Economic Surveys: Japan 2026"](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-economic-surveys-japan-2026_54cc833d-en/full-report/key-policy-insights_e3384128.html)). Preliminary outcomes for the 2026 wage round imply a further 5.1% increase for total wages and 3.5% for base pay, per the same OECD survey -- suggesting the pattern is continuing rather than being a one-off post-pandemic catch-up.

## What this means for household spending, with real numbers attached

The consumer-facing evidence of this wage pressure is concrete rather than theoretical. Family Income and Expenditure Survey data from Japan's own Statistics Bureau found average monthly consumption expenditure for households of two or more persons reaching 313,977 yen in August, up 5.5% in nominal terms and 2.3% in real terms year-on-year -- the fourth consecutive month of growth, with the increase specifically attributed to higher spending on cars and leisure activities ([Statistics Bureau of Japan, cited via Japan International Labour Foundation, October 2025](https://www.jilaf.or.jp/en/news/20251017-5793/)). Average monthly household income over the same period rose 6.0% nominally, though it fell 2.8% in real terms as inflation ran ahead of the income gain -- a genuinely important nuance that separates "wages are rising" from "households are getting ahead of inflation," which the data suggests is not uniformly true.

Minimum wages provide a further, specific data point: the nationwide average hourly minimum wage rose 66 yen (6.3%) to 1,121 yen, the largest increase on record, with the rate surpassing 1,000 yen in all 47 prefectures for the first time ever ([Japan International Labour Foundation, October 2025](https://www.jilaf.or.jp/en/news/20251017-5793/)). In 39 prefectures, local minimum wage councils actually set rates above the reference amount recommended by the national Central Minimum Wage Council -- a detail the same reporting attributes to local government concern about worker outflows to neighbouring prefectures offering higher pay, itself a direct symptom of how acute the shortage has become at the regional level.

## The structural response reshaping how firms compete for staff

Beyond wages, Japan's labour shortage is driving specific, documented shifts in how firms recruit and retain staff. A record 2,571,037 foreign workers were employed in Japan as of 31 October 2025, an increase of 268,450 (11.7%) year-on-year and the highest count since notification became mandatory in 2007 -- though the growth rate itself slowed from 12.4% the prior year, suggesting the foreign-worker pipeline, while still expanding, may not be accelerating fast enough to close the gap on its own ([Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, cited via Makerstations](https://www.makerstations.io/employment-in-japan-statistics/)). Workplaces employing foreign staff rose 8.5% to 371,215, with specialised and technical visa holders the fastest-growing single category, adding 146,776 people.

For mid-career professional roles specifically, the shortage has created what one recruitment-focused analysis describes as a "talent desert" -- particularly for candidates in their 30s and 40s with business-level English proficiency needed to serve as effective links to global headquarters, with top-tier candidates reportedly managing three to four simultaneous job offers, extending hiring cycles as firms compete directly on speed and terms ([Makana Partners, "The New Rules of Recruitment in Japan's Tightening Labor Market," August 2025](https://www.makanapartners.com/the-new-rules-of-recruitment-in-japans-tightening-labor-market)).

## The demographic backdrop that makes this a structural shift, not a cycle

It's worth being precise about why this labour shortage should be read as structural rather than cyclical, since that distinction changes how a business should plan around it. Japan's working-age population peaked in 1995 and has declined continuously since, with individuals aged 65 and older reaching a record 36.25 million in 2024, accounting for 29.3% of the total population ([Makana Partners, "The New Rules of Recruitment in Japan's Tightening Labor Market"](https://www.makanapartners.com/the-new-rules-of-recruitment-in-japans-tightening-labor-market)). Japan's total fertility rate sits around 1.2 births per woman, far below the replacement level -- meaning the labour supply constraint driving both the wage growth and the bankruptcy pattern described above is not a temporary post-pandemic adjustment that will ease as conditions normalise, but a multi-decade demographic trajectory that will continue independent of the broader business cycle. The Bank of Japan's own Tankan survey diffusion index for employment conditions -- a net balance measure of firms reporting labour shortage versus surplus -- registered at -35 across all industries as of recent readings, among the most acute readings in three decades, according to the OECD's Japan analysis.

## What this means for wage-cost planning specifically

A further, more specific consequence for any business budgeting Japan operations: the wage growth documented here shows genuine signs of continuing rather than being a one-off catch-up. The OECD's preliminary read on the 2026 Shunto round -- 5.1% total wages, 3.5% base pay -- sits close enough to 2025's actual outcome (5.25% headline, 3.7% base) that a business planning multi-year Japan cost projections should treat continued wage growth in the 3-5% range as the realistic baseline scenario rather than an anomaly likely to revert, particularly for roles in the specific sectors -- construction, logistics, elderly care, and mid-career professional positions requiring English fluency -- where the shortage is most acute.

## What this means for a Japan market strategy

The evidence gathered here supports treating Japan's labour shortage as a genuine two-sided story rather than a pure cost or productivity headwind. For a consumer-facing business, the wage and minimum-wage data specifically indicate real, broadening spending power -- not confined to major cities, given the minimum-wage increases hit all 47 prefectures, and not confined to large-firm employees, given the SME wage-growth spread the OECD documents. For a business trying to staff a Japan operation, the same underlying shortage is a genuine constraint that shows up concretely in bankruptcy data for labour-intensive sectors and in extended hiring cycles for skilled roles, meaning a market-entry plan that assumes Japan's traditionally lower-cost, high-availability labour market is not accounting for the structural shift the 2026 data shows. The two effects are connected rather than contradictory: the same shortage driving up bankruptcies in construction and logistics is the direct mechanism behind the wage growth showing up in household spending data.

## What a peer-reviewed study actually measured

Declining survey data quality is often attributed, somewhat vaguely, to "respondent fatigue" as if it were an external condition researchers simply have to accept. A peer-reviewed study published in Scientific Reports, examining straightlining behaviour across domains of social media use in a large dataset (the LifeOnSoMe study), provides more specific evidence than that framing usually allows. The study distinguishes between valid straightlining — genuinely consistent answers where a respondent's true attitudes are uniform across related items — and invalid straightlining, which reflects low-effort responding driven by "satisficing," where high cognitive demand or low motivation leads respondents to bypass careful consideration and adopt pattern-based shortcuts ([Nature Scientific Reports, "Straightlining prevalence across domains of social media use..."](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-14276-6)). This is a genuine peer-reviewed primary source, and its finding is specific and actionable: satisficing prevalence increases as task difficulty rises (longer questionnaires, more complex formats), as respondent ability decreases, and as respondent motivation wanes — three distinct, separately addressable levers rather than a single undifferentiated "fatigue."

The same study confirms a pattern noted elsewhere in the literature: straightlining is more common among younger respondents, a finding the paper notes is "particularly prevalent" and consistent with prior research. This has a direct implication for any research programme skewing toward a younger target demographic — a pattern relevant to much of Southeast Asia's population structure — since it suggests standard matrix-question design may be systematically less reliable for exactly the segment many regional studies are trying hardest to understand.

## What the broader academic literature adds

A separate, specifically cited academic study — Jeong, Aggarwal, Robinson, Kumar, Spearot, and Park, "Exhaustive or exhausting? Evidence on respondent fatigue in long surveys," published in the Journal of Development Economics (2023) — is referenced in a recent conversational-interview research paper as prior peer-reviewed evidence on this exact question ([arXiv preprint citing Jeong et al. 2023 in its reference list](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.11988)). This article did not independently retrieve the Jeong et al. paper itself, so its specific findings are flagged **Partially Verified** — it is cited here only as evidence that peer-reviewed economics literature has directly studied respondent fatigue in long surveys as a distinct research question, corroborating that this is a well-established academic concern rather than only an industry-commentary talking point.

## What separates well-documented design fixes from vendor marketing

A substantial amount of the material available on survey fatigue comes from survey-platform vendors with a product to sell, and their specific numeric claims (exact percentage completion-rate drops at exact question counts, for instance) should be treated with more caution than the peer-reviewed academic findings above. That said, several structural design principles are corroborated consistently across both the academic and industry-practitioner material reviewed for this article: matrices exceeding roughly eight to ten items show measurably higher straightlining rates; reverse-worded items function as a detection mechanism for inattentive responding but do not themselves prevent it (a genuinely fatigued respondent will straightline through a reverse-coded item without noticing the inconsistency); and randomising question and matrix order reduces the pattern-following behaviour that drives straightlining. These are design-level, not respondent-level, interventions.

## Why the framing matters for accountability, not just methodology

The Scientific Reports study's core distinction — that satisficing prevalence rises specifically with task difficulty, declining respondent ability, and waning motivation — reframes "respondent fatigue" from an inherent property of a panel population into a set of specific, diagnosable failure conditions that a questionnaire's design either triggers or avoids. This matters beyond methodology, because attributing poor data quality to "fatigue" in a client-facing report implicitly locates the problem outside the research team's control, when the peer-reviewed evidence reviewed here indicates the more common and more addressable cause sits in the questionnaire's own construction: excessive matrix length, cognitively demanding wording, or poor sequencing of easy versus difficult items.

## What this means for questionnaire design and client reporting

The defensible, evidence-supported standard is a specific audit rather than a general caution: before fielding, check matrix length against the roughly eight-to-ten-item threshold the literature associates with elevated straightlining risk; check whether reverse-worded items are present as a detection mechanism (understanding they detect rather than prevent the problem); and check where in the questionnaire the most cognitively demanding items sit relative to where respondent attention is freshest. When a fielded study does show elevated straightlining or satisficing indicators, the Scientific Reports evidence supports diagnosing it against these three specific levers — task difficulty, respondent ability match, and motivation — rather than defaulting to a general statement about "survey fatigue" that neither the researcher nor the client can act on.

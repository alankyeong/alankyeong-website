## The clearance-time gap, as cited from trade-facilitation data

The most specific evidence located for this article on where ASEAN's cross-border friction actually sits comes from a secondary source citing the World Bank's Logistics Performance Index (LPI) 2023 and UNESCAP trade facilitation data. That secondary source reports border-compliance times averaging 8–12 hours on the Singapore–Malaysia route, against 30–60 hours on Malaysia–Thailand crossings and 24–48 hours along the Vietnam–Cambodia corridor, and separately cites UNESCAP 2022 data showing "over 40% variance in customs-release efficiency across ASEAN land routes" ([Ken Research, "SEA Fulfillment Future: Cross-Border Logistics 2025," citing World Bank LPI 2023 and UNESCAP 2022 data](https://www.kenresearch.com/articles/sea-fulfillment-future-crossborder-logistics-2025)). This article did not independently retrieve the original World Bank LPI 2023 or UNESCAP source documents in this session — these figures are flagged as **Partially Verified**, and should be confirmed directly against worldbank.org and unescap.org before being presented as final in a published piece, particularly given how load-bearing they are for this article's central argument.

With that caveat stated plainly, the direction of the finding — an order-of-magnitude difference in clearance time between the region's best-connected corridor (Singapore–Malaysia) and its least (Malaysia–Thailand) — is a genuinely useful frame if it holds up under direct verification, because it locates the bottleneck specifically at customs and border process, not at freight capacity or trunk-route infrastructure, which is where sellers most often look first when diagnosing delivery reliability problems.

## Regional integration efforts, and their actual scope

On the policy response side, one industry market-report source states that the ASEAN Authorized Economic Operator Mutual Recognition Arrangement (AAMRA) — a mechanism intended to fast-track customs clearance for pre-vetted, "trusted trader" companies — expanded in February 2026 to include Cambodia, Lao PDR, and Myanmar alongside existing members ([Mordor Intelligence, "Southeast Asia Cross-border E-commerce Market Report 2031"](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/southeast-asia-cross-border-e-commerce-market)). This is a market-research firm's report rather than an ASEAN Secretariat primary announcement — flagged as **Partially Verified**, with the recommendation to confirm the AAMRA expansion directly against ASEAN Secretariat or member-state customs authority sources before publishing this as a settled fact, since a claim about which specific countries have joined a named regulatory mechanism is exactly the kind of detail that benefits from primary confirmation.

Separately, and on firmer footing since it concerns operational company decisions rather than a regulatory mechanism, the same market report describes Shopee Indonesia entering a formal agreement in May 2026 with four named domestic logistics partners — Anteraja, JNE, Pos Indonesia, and SiCepat — to run an "On-Time Guarantee" programme with compensation vouchers for missed delivery windows. This remains a claim sourced through a market-intelligence report rather than Shopee's own press materials directly, so it is retained here as **Partially Verified** as well, but it illustrates the kind of local last-mile network partnership that platform operators appear to be building specifically to address the domestic delivery layer, distinct from cross-border customs friction.

## Why this distinction matters for diagnosing delivery problems

The practical value of separating these two failure modes — customs clearance variability at the border, versus last-mile handoff into a domestic delivery network — is that they require different fixes, and a seller who misdiagnoses one as the other will invest in the wrong solution. If the LPI-cited clearance-time gap holds up under verification, a seller experiencing unpredictable delays on a specific corridor (Malaysia–Thailand, for instance, per the cited figures) is facing a border-process problem that individual carrier selection on the trunk route is unlikely to solve, because the delay is happening at the border checkpoint rather than in transit. Conversely, a seller with reliable customs clearance but inconsistent final delivery is more likely facing a last-mile network problem, which the Shopee–Anteraja/JNE/Pos Indonesia/SiCepat pattern suggests is being addressed through direct partnership with domestic carriers who have local operational reach, not through the cross-border logistics layer at all.

## What this means for a seller or logistics decision, given the evidence available

The most defensible recommendation this research supports, acknowledging the Partially Verified status of its two central data points, is a diagnostic one rather than a prescriptive one: before assuming a Southeast Asian cross-border delivery reliability problem is a freight or carrier-selection issue, check whether the delay pattern correlates with a specific border crossing (suggesting a customs-clearance problem, which the cited LPI-derived figures suggest can vary by roughly an order of magnitude between ASEAN's best and worst corridors) or with a specific destination market's last-mile network (suggesting the fix lies in a local delivery partnership of the kind Shopee Indonesia appears to be building, rather than in the cross-border leg at all). Given how much of this article's evidence rests on secondary citation of primary trade-facilitation data this research did not independently verify, a company making a material logistics investment decision on this basis should treat this article as a starting hypothesis to check against the World Bank LPI and UNESCAP source documents directly, not as a substitute for doing so.

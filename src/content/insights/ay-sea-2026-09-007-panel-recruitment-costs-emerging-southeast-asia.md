## A more limited evidence base than the topic usually gets credit for

This article set out to quantify the cost difference between urban and rural or offline respondent recruitment across Southeast Asian research markets — a gap frequently asserted in industry commentary but rarely evidenced with disclosed, comparable figures. Having searched for that evidence directly, the honest finding is that published, methodologically transparent data on this specific comparison is thin. Most of what is publicly available is vendor pricing pages (useful for general cost orientation, not for a rigorous urban-versus-rural comparison) or global benchmarks not specific to Southeast Asia. This section states that limitation plainly rather than presenting vendor marketing copy as settled fact.

## What is verifiably published

The clearest disclosed benchmark located in this research comes from Vase.ai, a Southeast Asia-headquartered research platform, whose sample-size and pricing guide states it draws on "500+ projects conducted across Southeast Asia, combined with ESOMAR guidelines and industry benchmarks," with figures last updated February 2026 ([Vase.ai, "Survey Sample Size: How Many Respondents Do You Actually Need?"](https://vase.ai/resources/blogs/survey-sample-size-guide)). This is a named platform disclosing its own aggregated project data — closer to primary than most sources reviewed here, though it remains a single vendor's book of business rather than an independent industry-wide dataset, and the guide's specific urban-versus-rural cost breakdown was not captured in the material retrieved for this article.

A second, more general source is a market-cost benchmark aggregator that cites method-based global ranges — in-depth interviews at US$5,000–15,000 per 10–15 interviews, focus groups at US$7,000–20,000+ per group, with participant incentives commonly cited at US$75–150 per person — while explicitly noting "these figures are not specific to Southeast Asia" and should be treated as "global reference ranges rather than local guarantees" ([marketresearchsoutheastasia.com, "Cost of Market Research Southeast Asia 2026 Report"](https://marketresearchsoutheastasia.com/insights/articles/how-much-does-market-research-cost-in-southeast-asia-a-clear-2026-budget-benchmark-for-confident-planning)). The source's own caveat is the most useful thing about it: even a publication whose entire purpose is Southeast Asia cost benchmarking states plainly that its core figures are not regionally specific.

On the general-population versus specialist-panel cost differential — a related but distinct question from the urban-rural one — a third source cites published panel-cost benchmarks of roughly US$2–15 per general-population survey complete via third-party marketplace sample, against US$15–60 per complete for an agency-owned, purpose-built panel, with harder-to-reach audiences (incidence below 5%) costing an estimated 8–15 times the base cost-per-interview according to one cited 2026 vendor guide ([ongraph.com, "Owned Research Panel vs Third-Party Sample Providers"](https://www.ongraph.com/owned-research-panel-vs-sample-providers/)). This source is a blog aggregating and citing other vendor guides rather than presenting first-party data — treat these specific multiples as **Partially Verified**, useful for directional orientation but not as a number to build a client-facing budget on without independent confirmation.

## The ESOMAR framework as the more durable reference point

The most genuinely authoritative material found in this research is not a cost figure at all — it is ESOMAR's "37 Questions" framework, the industry's own standard for what a sample provider should disclose about how its respondents were recruited, including questions on sample blending, the proportion of proprietary versus third-party sourced respondents, and offline recruitment method disclosure. ESOMAR is a genuine primary source here: a global professional association for market research, founded in 1947, whose code of practice is jointly maintained with the International Chamber of Commerce ([ESOMAR directory and organisational background](https://directory.esomar.org/service/10-recruiting/); [Wikipedia summary of ESOMAR's ICC/ESOMAR code history, cross-checked against ESOMAR's own site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESOMAR)). Several Southeast Asian panel providers reviewed in this research (TGM Research, Macromill South East Asia) explicitly publish or offer their own completed ESOMAR 37 responses, which is the closer-to-primary evidence a research buyer can actually act on: not "what does rural recruitment cost across the region," which no source here answers rigorously, but "will this specific vendor disclose its ESOMAR 37 answers for this specific market," which is a directly verifiable yes/no question.

## What this means for a research buyer, given the actual evidence available

The responsible conclusion from this research is narrower than the article originally set out to establish. Rather than asserting a specific cost multiple for reaching rural or lower-income Southeast Asian respondents — a claim the earlier, less rigorously sourced version of this article made without adequate evidence — the defensible position is: published, comparable pricing data on this specific question is not readily available, and any specific multiple currently circulating in industry commentary (including in earlier drafts of this article) should be treated as an estimate pending better evidence, not a citable fact. The more actionable step for a research buyer commissioning multi-market Southeast Asian fieldwork is not to ask a vendor for a number this article cannot itself verify, but to request the vendor's completed ESOMAR 37 disclosure and read it directly — since that is the one artefact in this evidence base that is both standardised and independently verifiable.

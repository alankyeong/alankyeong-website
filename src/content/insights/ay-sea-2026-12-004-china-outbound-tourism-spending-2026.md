## The growth forecast, and where it concentrates

Mainland Chinese travellers are projected to take 165 million to 175 million outbound trips in 2026 -- including Hong Kong and long-haul destinations -- up from an estimated 155 million in 2025, according to travel marketing and technology firm China Trading Desk ([South China Morning Post, "China's outbound travel set to soar by 10 million trips in 2026 -- but halve in Japan," 11 January 2026](https://www.scmp.com/economy/china-economy/article/3339348/chinas-outbound-travel-set-soar-10-million-trips-2026-halve-japan)). The same forecast identifies South Korea, Vietnam, and Thailand -- in that specific order -- as the three most popular destinations for 2026, citing convenient travel connections and established sightseeing infrastructure as the deciding factors, alongside a stronger yuan making outbound travel more affordable.

Separate industry estimates put total 2026 outbound spending at roughly US$280-300 billion, which would keep China as the world's largest outbound tourism spender by a wide margin ([Chinese Tourists Agency, "How Much Do Chinese Tourists Spend Abroad in 2026?," citing UNWTO outbound tourism data](https://chinesetouristagency.com/chinese-tourists-will-spend-over-255-billion-oversea/)). The same analysis notes that free independent travel (FIT, as distinct from group tours) now accounts for over 70% of outbound trips, with FIT travellers generating roughly six times the booking volume of group tours -- a structural shift in how this spending actually flows through to destination economies, favouring businesses set up to serve independent travellers over those built around the group-tour model that dominated a decade ago.

## The Japan exception, and why it's a policy story, not a demand story

The detail that makes this forecast genuinely more useful than a generic "outbound recovery continues" headline is the specific exception SCMP's reporting flags: visits to Japan could plummet by nearly 50% in 2026 amid a political dispute between Beijing and Tokyo, according to the same analysts and industry insiders cited in the China Trading Desk forecast. This is worth underscoring because it changes how a Japan-focused tourism or retail business should interpret a decline in Chinese visitor numbers: this is not a signal of weakening Chinese consumer demand for outbound travel in general -- the aggregate forecast shows continued growth -- but a market-specific effect tied to a bilateral diplomatic issue, meaning Chinese demand that would otherwise have gone to Japan is likely being redirected toward the destinations the forecast identifies as gaining share instead, primarily South Korea, Vietnam, and Thailand.

## The infrastructure enabling the growth, from China's own side

China's own visa-free and tourism-facilitation policy is a documented driver behind the outbound growth story, alongside its role in boosting inbound tourism. By late January 2026, China's immigration authorities had processed 697 million inbound and outbound crossings in 2025, a 14.2% increase and an all-time high, according to data presented at the Sino-European Dialogue in Shanghai ([China-US Focus, "China's Visa-Free Opening: Welcoming the World, Shaping the Narrative," 26 February 2026](https://www.chinausfocus.com/finance-economy/chinas-visafree-opening-welcoming-the-world-shaping-the-narrative)). Outbound travel by mainland Chinese residents specifically reached 168 million trips in the same dataset -- broadly consistent with, though not identical to, the China Trading Desk forecast cited above, a discrepancy likely explained by different reporting periods and methodologies between the two sources, worth flagging rather than treating as a single settled figure.

Payment infrastructure has also scaled to support this outbound spending pattern directly: integration of Chinese digital payment platforms (Alipay, WeChat Pay) with overseas merchants has seen a reported 37% surge, meaning Chinese travellers increasingly do not need foreign currency or internationally accepted credit cards to spend at destination, from luxury retail to street food vendors ([Travel And Tour World, "The Return of the Dragon," 4 June 2026](https://www.travelandtourworld.com/news/article/o4u3eadmrn69/)) -- a specific, practical detail for any destination business evaluating whether it's actually positioned to capture Chinese outbound spending, since payment friction is a genuinely removable barrier that some businesses have not yet addressed.

## The AI-driven shift in how this spending gets planned and captured

A structural change in how Chinese outbound travel is planned deserves separate attention because it affects which destination businesses actually capture the growth described above. Trip.com Group, one of the region's dominant online travel platforms, showcased a joint initiative with Google at the World Travel Market in November 2025 specifically aimed at using AI and data insights to map what the same reporting describes as a shift in Chinese traveller motivation from "escape-based" travel to "connection-based" travel -- a reframing with direct implications for which kinds of destination experiences and marketing messages actually convert ([Future Market Insights, "China Outbound Travel Market," 16 February 2026](https://www.futuremarketinsights.com/reports/china-outbound-travel-market)). The same source notes Chinese investors had established over 50,000 overseas enterprises across 190 countries by end-2025, according to Ministry of Commerce spokesperson He Yongqian -- a scale of outbound Chinese business presence that increasingly shapes destination-market infrastructure (Chinese-language services, familiar payment rails, culturally attuned hospitality) well beyond pure tourism spending itself.

## Why the destination mix reflects convenience and connectivity more than novelty

It's worth being explicit about why South Korea, Vietnam, and Thailand specifically lead the 2026 destination ranking, since the reasoning has direct implications for which other markets might see similar growth. James Chin, a professor of Asian studies at the University of Tasmania, attributes the pattern to convenient travel connections and an abundance of established sightseeing attractions in each of the three leading destinations, rather than to any single novel attraction or marketing campaign -- alongside noting that Chinese travel agencies were "becoming more adventurous" in the destinations they were willing to package and promote ([South China Morning Post, 11 January 2026](https://www.scmp.com/economy/china-economy/article/3339348/chinas-outbound-travel-set-soar-10-million-trips-2026-halve-japan)). The same reporting attributes part of the growth specifically to a strengthening yuan, which rose more than 1% against the US dollar in the month preceding the report -- a currency effect that could plausibly reverse and dampen the growth forecast if the yuan's trajectory shifts, a genuine forward-looking uncertainty this article's underlying forecast does not appear to stress-test explicitly.

## What this means for destination markets and businesses

For businesses in South Korea, Vietnam, and Thailand specifically, the evidence supports treating 2026 as a year of genuine, forecast-confirmed inbound Chinese visitor growth, with the FIT shift meaning independent-traveller-oriented services (rather than group-tour infrastructure alone) are likely to capture a disproportionate share of that spending. For Japan-focused businesses, the more urgent planning question is not whether Chinese visitor spending will recover broadly -- it appears set to, elsewhere -- but how exposed a specific business is to the bilateral dispute's effect on Japan visitation specifically, and whether that exposure needs to be actively managed (through diversifying visitor-source-market marketing, for instance) rather than assumed to be a temporary blip that will resolve on its own timeline. Given the specific 165 million to 175 million trip range this forecast spans -- itself a reasonably wide band for a single-year projection -- businesses making significant capacity or marketing-spend decisions off this outlook should build in enough flexibility to accommodate the forecast landing anywhere within that range rather than treating the midpoint as a firm commitment.
